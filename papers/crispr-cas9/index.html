CRISPR-Cas9, translated

Author: Daniel Jordan Viray

Jinek et al., Science 337 (6096): 816–821, 2012 · science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1225829
Senior authors Jennifer Doudna & Emmanuelle Charpentier — Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2020.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can try the mechanism yourself instead of taking its word for it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Bacteria have an immune system. When a virus attacks, they file away a mugshot of its DNA — and if it ever comes back, they send out molecular scissors to cut the invader's DNA. In 2012, Doudna, Charpentier and colleagues worked out exactly how the scissors operate: a protein called Cas9, guided by two small RNA molecules — one is the address matching the target, the other is the handle Cas9 grips. They showed Cas9 only cuts next to a short DNA "password" (NGG), snipping both strands 3 letters before it. Then the famous trick: fuse the two RNAs into one, and you can reprogram the scissors to cut any DNA sequence — just change the address. That single insight became the CRISPR gene-editing revolution.

The translation

1 · The setup — from the Introduction

The paper says

adaptive immunity against viruses

Jinek et al., Science 2012 — on what CRISPR does for bacteria

What it means

Bacteria get sick too — viruses attack them. So bacteria evolved an immune system with a memory: when a virus invades, they file away a short mugshot of its DNA (called a spacer).

If that virus ever returns, the mugshot guides the defense to recognize and destroy it. CRISPR is that filing system. Cas9 is the enforcer.

2 · The scissors need two handles — from the Results

The paper says

forms a two-RNA structure that directs the CRISPR-associated protein Cas9 to introduce double-stranded breaks in target DNA

Jinek et al., Science 2012

What it means

Cas9 is the scissors, but it can't work alone — it needs two RNA molecules. One (crRNA) carries the 20-letter address matching the target. The other (tracrRNA) is the handle Cas9 grips.

The paper proved the handle is non-negotiable: crRNA alone does nothing, but add tracrRNA and Cas9 starts cutting. Together they form a two-RNA homing device that directs Cas9 to snap both strands of the matching DNA.

3 · The password — from the Results

The paper says

the PAM conforms to an NGG consensus sequence

Jinek et al., Science 2012

What it means

Cas9 doesn't cut everywhere the address matches — it also demands a password next to the target: the letters NGG (any letter, then two G's), called the PAM.

No password, no cut — the paper showed mutating either G kills the cutting. This is also how bacteria avoid shredding their own DNA: their filing cabinet stores mugshots without the password next to them.

4 · The cut — from the Results

The paper says

blunt ends at a position three base pairs upstream of the PAM

Jinek et al., Science 2012

What it means

When address and password both check out, Cas9 snips both DNA strands 3 letters before the password, leaving clean blunt ends (no overhangs).

Two separate blades do the work: the HNH domain cuts the strand matching the address, the RuvC domain cuts the opposite strand. The paper proved it by disabling each blade — the DNA only got nicked on one side. And it's fast: about as quick as the restriction enzymes labs already used.

Data slide · the seed rule

The paper's mismatch experiments, simplified: click letters in the target row to introduce mismatches against the guide. Break the ~13 letters next to the password (the seed) and cutting dies; mismatches far from the password are tolerated. Illustrative sequence.

Rule of thumb from the paper: the 13 password-adjacent letters must match; up to 6 contiguous mismatches at the far end are tolerated.

5 · The payoff — from the Conclusions

The paper says

potential to exploit the system for RNA-programmable genome editing

Jinek et al., Science 2012

What it means

Then the famous trick: fuse the two RNAs into a single "guide RNA" — address at the front, handle at the back — and it cuts just as well.

Now reprogramming the scissors means changing just 20 letters of RNA. They proved it by aiming it at five different spots in a jellyfish glow gene — all five cut exactly where intended. The authors saw it clearly: a simple, versatile, RNA-programmed system for editing genomes. Within a year, labs worldwide had it working in human cells.

Key terms, in one line each

CRISPR — the bacterium's filing system: mugshots of past viral invaders stored in its DNA.
Cas9 — the protein scissors that cut DNA where the guide RNA points.
crRNA — the address label: 20 letters matching the target DNA.
tracrRNA — the handle: the RNA piece Cas9 grips to become active.
sgRNA — "single guide RNA": the two RNAs fused into one programmable molecule.
PAM — the password (NGG): a short DNA motif that must sit next to the target for cutting.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

This was a test-tube study — purified Cas9 cutting DNA in a dish. Making it work inside human cells came the next year (2013). Also: this is S. pyogenes Cas9 with its NGG password; other bacteria's versions use different passwords. And the paper is about the cutting mechanism, not a how-to for editing — the Nobel Prize (2020, Doudna & Charpentier) was for the discovery.
