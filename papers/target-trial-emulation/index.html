Target trial emulation, translated

Author: Daniel Jordan Viray

Hernán & Robins, American Journal of Epidemiology 183 (8): 758–764, 2016 · doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwv254
The framework paper behind modern real-world evidence (RWE).

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim — including a simulation where you can watch a famous bias manufacture a fake drug benefit, then watch the paper's fix delete it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Randomized trials are the gold standard for "does this treatment work?" — but often you can't run one: too slow, too expensive, or unethical. So researchers mine health records instead, and keep fooling themselves: observational studies swore hormone therapy protected women's hearts, until a real trial found no such thing. Hernán & Robins' fix is disarmingly simple: before touching the data, write down the trial you would run — who gets in, what the strategies are, when the clock starts — then emulate every piece of that protocol with the observational data. Specifying the "target trial" forces start times and eligibility to line up, which quietly deletes the self-inflicted biases (like immortal time bias) that made the records lie.

The translation

1 · The problem — from the Abstract

The paper says

Causal inference from large observational databases (big data) can be viewed as an attempt to emulate a randomized experiment

Hernán & Robins, Am J Epidemiol 2016

What it means

Every study that mines medical records to ask "did the drug help?" is secretly pretending to be a randomized trial — one group "got" the treatment, another didn't, and we compare outcomes. The pretending is usually sloppy: nobody wrote down the rules of the imaginary trial, so the groups end up unfairly matched in hidden ways.

The paper's move: stop pretending accidentally. Pretend on purpose, rigorously.

2 · The idea — from the Abstract

The paper says

makes the target trial explicit

Hernán & Robins, Am J Epidemiol 2016 — on what their framework does

What it means

Step one is not statistics — it's writing a protocol, exactly like a real trial's: who is eligible, what the treatment strategies are, how assignment works, when follow-up starts ("time zero"), what outcome counts, and how it will be analyzed.

If you can't state the trial, you don't have a well-posed question yet. Once the protocol exists, each piece gets an observational stand-in — and the gaps become visible instead of silently corrupting the answer.

3 · Why the alignment matters — from the paper

The paper says

the target experiment or target trial—that would answer the question of interest

Hernán & Robins, Am J Epidemiol 2016

What it means

Here's the trap the protocol disarms. Patients start treatment after entering the data — days or months later. To start a drug at month 6, you must survive to month 6. If an analysis counts those first 6 months as "treated time," it hands the treatment group free, guaranteed-alive months the control group never gets. The drug looks protective even if it's sugar pills. That's immortal time bias.

A real trial can't have this bug — everyone's clock starts at randomization. Emulating the trial (aligning time zero) removes the bug by construction.

4 · The payoff — from the Abstract

The paper says

helps avoid common methodologic pitfalls

Hernán & Robins, Am J Epidemiol 2016

What it means

The hormone-therapy reversal is the poster child: records said hormone therapy protected hearts; the Women's Health Initiative trial said otherwise; and when Hernán's group re-analyzed the records as a trial (2008), the fake protection largely evaporated — the discrepancy was design, not biology.

Today this framework is the backbone of real-world evidence: how regulators and payers decide what claims and EHR data can — and cannot — honestly say about whether treatments work.

Key terms, in one line each

Target trial — the hypothetical randomized trial your observational study is emulating, protocol and all.
Time zero — the moment eligibility, treatment assignment, and follow-up all start — aligned.
Immortal time bias — follow-up time during which the outcome can't happen by design, falsely credited to treatment.
Confounding — group differences (sicker patients get the drug) that masquerade as treatment effects.
Estimand — the precisely defined effect you're estimating: of starting treatment, or of sticking with it?
RWE — real-world evidence: causal conclusions drawn from routine data (claims, EHRs) instead of trials.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

Emulation disciplines the design; it cannot manufacture data that were never collected. If a key confounder (say, frailty) isn't in the records, no protocol can adjust for it — the framework makes that gap explicit rather than hiding it, but the gap remains. It also demands rich, well-timed data: vague treatment dates reintroduce exactly the misalignment the method exists to remove.
