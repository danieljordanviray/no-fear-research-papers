AlphaFold 3, translated

Author: Daniel Jordan Viray

Abramson et al., Nature 630: 493–500, 2024 · nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07487-w
From Google DeepMind & Isomorphic Labs — the follow-up to the Nobel-winning AlphaFold 2.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can see the method work instead of taking its word for it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

AlphaFold 2 (2020) learned to fold proteins — predict a protein's 3D shape from its letters. But cells aren't lone proteins: they're proteins hugging DNA, RNA, drug molecules, and metal ions. AlphaFold 3 is the upgrade that models the whole scene at once — any mix of proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, and ions — and beats the specialized tools built for each individual job. The engine changed too: instead of assembling a structure piece by piece, AF3 starts from a cloud of random atom positions and "denoises" it into the final structure — the same trick behind AI image generators. For drug discovery, that's the dream input: show it a protein and a candidate drug, and it shows you how they'd fit together.

The translation

1 · The gap — from the Abstract

The paper says

predicting the joint structure of complexes including proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, ions and modified residues

Abramson et al., Nature 2024

What it means

AlphaFold 2 was a soloist: one protein, folded alone. But biology happens in complexes — a drug gripping its target protein, a protein reading a strand of DNA, an antibody latching onto a virus.

AlphaFold 3 predicts the whole group arrangement at once: proteins, DNA/RNA, small molecules (most drugs), ions, and chemically modified building blocks — in a single model.

2 · The new engine — from the Methods

The paper says

random noise is sampled and then recurrently denoised

Abramson et al., Nature 2024 — on the diffusion module

What it means

The structure engine is now diffusion — the same idea as AI image generators. Start with atoms scattered at random positions, like static on a screen. Then, step by step, the network "denoises" the cloud: at high noise it fixes the big picture (overall shape), at low noise it polishes the fine chemistry (exact bond angles).

Because it works directly on raw atom coordinates, it doesn't care what kind of molecule it's building — that's what makes the all-in-one trick possible.

3 · The results — from the Abstract

The paper says

far greater accuracy for protein–ligand interactions compared with state-of-the-art docking tools

Abramson et al., Nature 2024

What it means

They tested it on PoseBusters — 428 recently published protein–drug structures held back from training — and AF3 placed drugs into their binding pockets far more accurately than the classic docking software (Vina, Gold) the industry relied on.

Same story elsewhere: much better than nucleic-acid specialists at protein–DNA/RNA complexes, and substantially better than its own predecessor (AlphaFold-Multimer) at antibody–antigen pairs. One generalist beat the specialists at nearly every event.

4 · The payoff — from the Abstract

The paper says

modelling across biomolecular space is possible within a single unified deep-learning framework

Abramson et al., Nature 2024

What it means

Before: a drawer full of single-purpose tools, each mediocre. Now: one framework for nearly every structure question in molecular biology.

The practical payoff is drug discovery — screen candidate molecules against a target protein on a computer, see exactly how they'd bind, and only synthesize the promising ones. That's why the co-author lab is Isomorphic, a drug-discovery company: this model is a design tool, not just a prediction trophy.

Key terms, in one line each

Ligand — a small molecule (often a drug) that binds to a protein.
Docking — the older software approach: physically simulating how a drug might fit into a protein pocket.
Diffusion — generating a structure by gradually removing noise from random atom positions.
PoseBusters — a benchmark of 428 recent protein–drug structures used to grade predictions honestly.
RMSD < 2 Å — the pass mark: predicted atom positions within 2 ångströms (atomic-scale) of the truth.
pLDDT — the model's own confidence score per atom, so you know which parts to trust.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

Diffusion models can hallucinate: in floppy, unstructured regions AF3 may invent tidy-looking structure that isn't real (the team dampened this by training it partly on its predecessor's outputs). It also occasionally gets molecular handedness wrong — a chirality violation in 4.4% of benchmark cases. And a prediction, however confident, is still a hypothesis until an experiment confirms it.
