The ChatGPT productivity experiment, translated

Noy & Zhang, Science 381 (6654): 187–192, 2023 · science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586
A preregistered randomized experiment on generative AI and professional work.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can flip between "with ChatGPT" and "without" and watch the numbers move.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Months after ChatGPT launched, two researchers ran the cleanest possible test: recruit 453 working professionals — marketers, grant writers, consultants, data analysts, HR staff, managers — give them realistic writing tasks from their own occupations, and randomly assign half to use ChatGPT. The results were lopsided: the ChatGPT group finished 40% faster with work graded 18% better — and the gap between strong and weak writers shrank, because the weakest gained the most. Then the kicker: two weeks later, the exposed group was already using ChatGPT in their real jobs at twice the rate of the control group. The experiment escaped the lab.

The translation

1 · The setup — from the Abstract

The paper says

we assigned occupation-specific, incentivized writing tasks to 453 college-educated professionals and randomly exposed half of them to ChatGPT

Noy & Zhang, Science 2023

What it means

Every design choice here is doing work. Occupation-specific: a grant writer wrote a grant proposal, a marketer a press release — tasks people actually do. Incentivized: pay depended on quality, so nobody coasted. Randomly exposed: a coin flip decided who got ChatGPT, so the two groups were comparable — the gold-standard RCT design, preregistered in advance.

Each participant did a first task without AI (a baseline), then a second task where the treatment group had ChatGPT. Graders didn't know who used what.

2 · The result — from the Abstract

The paper says

The average time taken decreased by 40% and output quality rose by 18%

Noy & Zhang, Science 2023

What it means

Usually speed and quality trade off — faster means sloppier. Here both moved the right way: dramatically faster and noticeably better, as graded by blinded evaluators in the same occupations.

How people used it mattered: the best workflow wasn't pasting the whole task into the chatbot — it was drafting with AI, then editing. The productivity came from the human–AI loop, not the AI alone.

3 · The equalizer — from the Abstract

The paper says

Inequality between workers decreased

Noy & Zhang, Science 2023

What it means

Participants who were weak writers on the first (unaided) task gained enormously from ChatGPT; strong writers gained a little. The floor rose toward the ceiling.

If that pattern sounds familiar, it's the same signature as the 5,179-agent call-center study (translated elsewhere on this site): early evidence that generative AI compresses the skill distribution from below rather than stretching it — the opposite of the last technology wave.

4 · The escape — from the Abstract

The paper says

2 times as likely to report using it in their real job

Noy & Zhang, Science 2023 — two weeks after the experiment

What it means

Most experiments end when the session ends. This one followed participants out the door: two weeks later the ChatGPT-exposed group was using it at work at double the control group's rate; two months later, still 1.6 times.

Exposure itself was an intervention — a few hours of forced hands-on use converted skeptics into adopters. (The paper also notes excitement and anxiety about AI both rose temporarily — a very 2023 finding.)

Key terms, in one line each

Preregistered — the hypotheses and analysis plan were filed publicly before data collection, so the results can't be cherry-picked after the fact.
Random assignment — a coin flip, not self-selection, decided who got ChatGPT — that's what makes the comparison causal.
Incentivized tasks — participants were paid more for better work, mimicking real stakes.
Blinded grading — evaluators scored the writing without knowing whether AI was used.
Treatment / control — the group that got ChatGPT vs the group that worked unaided.
Effect decay — the adoption gap narrowed over two months (2× → 1.6×) as awareness spread to everyone.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

The tasks were short, self-contained writing jobs — the kind of work LLMs are best at — lasting tens of minutes, not weeks-long projects with stakeholders and ambiguity. The quality grades were holistic ratings by fellow professionals, not downstream business outcomes. And the study ran in early 2023 on the ChatGPT of that era; effects could shrink (as unaided baselines adapt) or grow (as models improve). It establishes what AI did to these tasks, not a law of labor markets.
