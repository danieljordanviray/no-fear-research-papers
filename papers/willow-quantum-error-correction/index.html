Quantum error correction, translated

Google Quantum AI and Collaborators, Nature 638: 920–926, 2025 · nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08449-y
The Willow chip paper — the first demonstration of quantum error correction below the threshold.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can run its key equation yourself instead of taking its word for it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Quantum computers are comically fragile: their bits (qubits) get scrambled by the slightest heat or stray field — roughly one error per thousand operations, while real applications need closer to one per ten billion. For thirty years, theory said there's a fix: spread one logical qubit across many physical ones, and if your hardware errors sit below a critical threshold, making the code bigger suppresses errors exponentially. Nobody had ever actually crossed that line. Google's Willow chip did: grids of physical qubits acting as single logical qubits whose memory lasted 2.4× longer than the best physical qubit inside them — and every step up in size cut the error rate by a factor of ~2.1, exactly as the theory promised. That's the moment quantum error correction went from math to engineering.

The translation

1 · The problem — from the Introduction

The paper says

no quantum processor has definitively shown below-threshold performance

Google Quantum AI, Nature 2025

What it means

A normal computer bit is rock-solid. A qubit is a soap bubble: heat, vibration, stray magnetism — anything can pop it. Today's best qubits still make an error roughly every thousand operations, and useful algorithms (simulating a new drug molecule, say) need errors rarer than one in ten billion.

Theorists had a rescue plan — error correction — but it only works if hardware errors are below a "threshold." Until this paper, no machine had definitively gotten there.

2 · The idea — from the Introduction

The paper says

the logical error rate should be suppressed exponentially as we increase the number of physical qubits per logical qubit

Google Quantum AI, Nature 2025

What it means

Don't build one perfect qubit — build a committee. Spread a single logical qubit across a grid of physical qubits (the surface code) that constantly cross-check each other, so a monitoring program (the decoder) can spot and account for errors without looking at the data itself.

The magic claim: once hardware errors are below the threshold, a bigger committee doesn't get noisier — it gets exponentially more reliable. Each step up in grid size (the "distance") divides the error rate by a factor called Λ (lambda).

Data slide · run the paper's equation

The paper measured Λ = 2.14: every time the code distance grows by 2, the logical error rate divides by ~2.14. 3 · The result — from the Results

The paper says

preserving quantum information for more than twice as long as its best constituent physical qubit

Google Quantum AI, Nature 2025 — on the distance-7 logical qubit

What it means

On the 105-qubit Willow chip, the distance-7 logical qubit — a committee of 97 qubits — remembered its state for 291 microseconds, versus 119 for the single best physical qubit in the machine (85 for a typical one). The committee beat its best member by 2.4×.

And crucially, performance improved with size exactly as predicted: error rates fell by a factor of Λ = 2.14 at each step (distance 3 → 5 → 7). They even kept it working while decoding errors in real time, on a cycle of just 1.1 microseconds.

Data slide · beyond breakeven

"Breakeven" means the error-corrected logical qubit outlives the best physical qubit you'd otherwise use. Willow's distance-7 memory, from the paper:

4 · The payoff — from the Results

The paper says

surpassed with a sufficiently large logical qubit

Google Quantum AI, Nature 2025

What it means

Below-threshold operation flips the engineering problem. Before: errors were a wall — bigger machines just failed faster. Now: reliability is something you buy with qubits, at a known exchange rate (Λ ≈ 2.14 per size step).

That's the on-ramp to fault-tolerant quantum computing — machines that could simulate molecules for drug discovery, design new materials and catalysts, and tackle optimization problems classical computers can't touch. Not this year, and not with 105 qubits — but the path is now an engineering roadmap instead of a hope.

Key terms, in one line each

Qubit — a quantum bit: can hold a blend of 0 and 1, and is destroyed by the slightest disturbance.
Logical qubit — one reliable qubit built from many fragile physical ones acting as a committee.
Surface code — the committee layout: data qubits on a grid, checker qubits constantly cross-examining them.
Code distance (d) — the committee's size; bigger d catches more errors, at 2d² − 1 physical qubits each.
Threshold — the hardware error rate below which growing the code helps instead of hurts.
Λ (lambda) — the payoff factor: how much the error rate divides by each time distance grows by 2. Willow: 2.14.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

This is a quantum memory result — storing a qubit reliably, the prerequisite step; actually computing with many logical qubits is the next milestone. The paper also found a spoiler: rare correlated error events (about once an hour, origin unknown) currently put a floor under how low errors can go (~10⁻¹⁰ in their repetition codes) — the slider above assumes that floor gets fixed. And note: Willow's famous "5 minutes vs 10²⁵ years" benchmark was a separate sampling demo from the same chip launch, not a result in this paper.
