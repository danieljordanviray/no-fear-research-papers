Prediction markets, translated

Wolfers & Zitzewitz, Journal of Economic Perspectives 18 (2): 107–126, 2004 · aeaweb.org — full citation & abstract
The founding explainer of prediction markets — the intellectual ancestor of Kalshi and Polymarket.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can watch the crowd's wisdom assemble itself.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

What if the best forecaster of an election isn't a pollster or a pundit, but a price? A prediction market is a betting market where contracts pay off based on future events — "pays $1 if she wins." The price is then a probability with money behind it. Wolfers & Zitzewitz surveyed the evidence in 2004 and found a pattern: across elections, movie box offices, football, and even corporate sales forecasts, market prices beat the polls, the experts, and the official forecasts — because trading forces thousands of scattered scraps of knowledge into a single number, and being wrong costs money. This is the paper that gave the idea its academic spine, twenty years before Kalshi and Polymarket made it mainstream.

The translation

1 · The idea — from the Abstract

The paper says

markets can be used to aggregate disperse information into efficient forecasts of uncertain future events

Wolfers & Zitzewitz, J. Econ. Perspectives 2004

What it means

Nobody knows the whole picture — but thousands of people each know a piece: a local organizer senses turnout, a studio employee hears test-screening buzz, a sales rep knows her region is soft.

A market is a machine for pooling those pieces. People with information bet; prices move until they reflect everything the crowd collectively knows. The price is the forecast — updated continuously, with real money punishing sloppy thinking.

2 · The instrument — from the Abstract

The paper says

yield insight into the market's expectations about probabilities, means and medians

Wolfers & Zitzewitz, J. Econ. Perspectives 2004 — on carefully designed contracts

What it means

Contract design is question design. A winner-take-all contract ("$1 if she wins") priced at $0.63 reads as a 63% probability. A vote-share contract priced at $0.52 reads as an expected 52% of the vote. Different contract shapes extract different statistics — probabilities, averages, medians — from the same crowd.

You can even ask conditional questions — "her vote share if she's the nominee" — creating "decision markets" meant to inform actual choices.

3 · The evidence — from the Abstract

The paper says

they outperform most moderately sophisticated benchmarks

Wolfers & Zitzewitz, J. Econ. Perspectives 2004

What it means

Not a theory — a track record. The Iowa Electronic Markets (a university-run election market, max bet $500) forecast US presidential vote shares with an average error of 1.6 percentage points, against 1.9 for the Gallup Poll across 13 candidacies — and over longer horizons the market's edge grows, because polls bounce around with the news cycle while prices stay anchored.

4 · The limit — from the Abstract

The paper says

we still have the usual problem of disentangling correlation from causation

Wolfers & Zitzewitz, J. Econ. Perspectives 2004

What it means

The paper's own example: in 2004, a decision market said Democrats would win the biggest vote share in the world where John Edwards was the nominee. But that world is one where Edwards surged for a reason — great campaigning, a message catching fire. Forcing the nomination doesn't import the surge.

A market answers "what do things look like if X happens?" — not "what happens if we make X happen?" Correlation versus causation: the same wall every observational method hits, honestly flagged by the authors themselves.

Key terms, in one line each

Contract — a bet with defined payoffs: e.g., $1 if an event happens, $0 if it doesn't; its price reads as a probability.
IEM — the Iowa Electronic Markets: the University of Iowa's research market (since 1988, capped at $500 per trader).
Aggregation — pooling many people's partial information into one number — here, via prices.
Decision market — a conditional market ("outcome if we choose X") built to advise a decision, not just forecast.
Favorite–longshot bias — the known quirk: markets slightly overprice longshots and underprice favorites.
Manipulation — trying to move prices for optics; the evidence says it fails — wrong prices are profit opportunities for everyone else.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

The IEM's traders were self-selected and unrepresentative — the miracle is that prices still worked. Markets need liquidity and well-posed questions; thin markets wobble, and ambiguous resolution ("who won?") can break them. And a great forecast is not a decision rule — see the correlation caveat above. The authors wrote before the modern era; the 2004-vintage evidence was mostly small-stakes markets.
