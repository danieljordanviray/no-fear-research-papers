ESM3, translated

Hayes et al., Science 387, 2025 · science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ads0018
From EvolutionaryScale — "Simulating 500 million years of evolution with a language model."

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can feel the numbers instead of taking its word for it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Proteins are written in a 20-letter language, and evolution has been writing sentences for over 3 billion years. ESM3 is a language model — a transformer, like the one from the Attention paper — trained on 2.78 billion protein sequences plus their 3D shapes and their jobs, until it learned the grammar of what makes a protein work. Because it understands sequence, structure, and function as three dialects of one language, you can prompt it with any mix: "here's a partial shape, here's the job — finish the protein." The headline proof: they asked it for a glowing protein (GFP), then built its answer in a real lab. It glowed — despite sharing only 58% of its letters with any known fluorescent protein, a gap the authors estimate nature would need ~500 million years of evolution to produce.

The translation

1 · The idea — from the Abstract

The paper says

language models trained at scale on evolutionary data can generate functional proteins that are far away from known proteins

Hayes et al., Science 2025

What it means

Treat evolution as an author and proteins as its sentences. Train a big enough language model on the whole library — billions of sentences — and it learns the grammar: which letter patterns fold, which ones glow, which ones catalyze.

Then it can write new sentences: working proteins that don't exist in nature and aren't close copies of anything that does.

2 · The model — from the Abstract

The paper says

reasons over the sequence, structure, and function of proteins

Hayes et al., Science 2025

What it means

Older protein models read only the letters. ESM3 reads three tracks at once — the sequence (letters), the structure (3D shape), and the function (job description) — all converted into tokens in one shared vocabulary.

That means prompts can mix dialects: give it half a shape and a keyword like "fluorescence," and it completes the rest. The flagship model has 98 billion parameters, trained on 771 billion tokens.

3 · The result — from the Abstract

The paper says

we found a bright fluorescent protein at a far distance (58% sequence identity) from known fluorescent proteins

Hayes et al., Science 2025

What it means

The test was deliberately hard: invent a new green fluorescent protein — the workhorse glow-in-the-dark protein from jellyfish, used in labs everywhere. They prompted ESM3 (with a step-by-step "chain of thought"), synthesized its designs in a real lab, and screened for glow.

One design, esmGFP, glowed bright green — while differing at roughly 100 of its 238 letter positions from the closest glowing protein nature ever made. Not a tweak of an existing protein: a genuinely distant relative, written from scratch.

4 · The payoff — from the Conclusions

The paper says

More than three billion years of evolution have produced an image of biology encoded into the space of natural proteins

Hayes et al., Science 2025

What it means

Evolution explores protein space one random step at a time, over geological time. A trained model can jump — the authors estimate esmGFP sits where ~500 million years of ordinary evolution would land.

The promise: proteins to order. Enzymes that digest plastic, binders that neutralize a virus, industrial catalysts — designed by prompting, instead of prospecting through nature or shuffling mutations for years in a lab.

Key terms, in one line each

Sequence identity — the share of letter positions that match between two proteins, as a percentage.
GFP — green fluorescent protein: nature's glow-in-the-dark protein, a lab workhorse since the 1990s.
Multimodal — one model reading several kinds of input at once — here: letters, shapes, and functions.
Tokens — the chunks a language model reads and writes; ESM3 tokenizes shape and function too, not just letters.
Chain of thought — prompting the model to generate step by step, which improved the GFP designs.
esmGFP — the paper's trophy: a bright, lab-verified fluorescent protein designed by the model.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

The "500 million years" figure is the authors' estimate, inferred from how fast natural fluorescent proteins diverge — evocative, not a stopwatch reading. One glowing protein is a proof of concept, not a drug pipeline: most generated proteins still fail, and every design needs lab validation. The flagship 98B model is available via API; the openly released version is much smaller. And capability cuts both ways — the release was deliberately staged with safety reviews.
