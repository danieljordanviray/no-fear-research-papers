Attention Is All You Need, translated

Vaswani et al., NeurIPS 2017 · arXiv:1706.03762 · arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762
Eight researchers at Google. Arguably the most influential AI paper ever published.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can see the mechanism work instead of taking its word for it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Before 2017, AI read language the way you read through a keyhole: one word at a time, in order, struggling to remember what came twenty words earlier. Eight Google researchers asked: what if a sentence could look at itself all at once — every word checking every other word to decide what matters? That mechanism is called attention. Their model, the Transformer, threw out the old one-word-at-a-time machinery entirely — and still translated better than anything before it, training in days instead of weeks. Every modern AI you know — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — and the architecture inside AlphaFold descends from this paper's blueprint.

The translation

1 · The old way — from the Introduction

The paper says

The dominant sequence transduction models are based on complex recurrent or convolutional neural networks

Vaswani et al., 2017

What it means

In 2017, the standard AI for language worked like a person reading with a flashlight in a dark room: one word at a time, in strict order ("recurrence"). Two problems: it's slow — you can't read word 20 until you've finished word 19, so training can't be parallelized — and it's forgetful: by the end of a long sentence, the beginning has faded.

2 · The bet — from the Abstract

The paper says

based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely

Vaswani et al., 2017

What it means

The bet, which sounded reckless at the time: delete the assembly line completely. No reading in order, no convolutions — just attention. Every word looks directly at every other word in the sentence and decides how much each one matters to it. Distance stops mattering: word 1 and word 40 are one hop apart.

3 · How attention works — from the Method

The paper says

mapping a query and a set of key-value pairs to an output

Vaswani et al., 2017 — the paper's definition of an attention function

What it means

Here's the whole trick. Every word produces three things: a query ("what am I looking for?"), a key ("what do I offer?"), and a value ("what I actually contribute"). A word compares its query against everyone's keys, and takes a weighted blend of the values of whoever matches.

So in "the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired," the word "it" learns to grab "animal" — that's how the model knows what "it" refers to. And it does this with 8 heads at once — eight different kinds of relationships (who did it, what it refers to, what modifies what…) in parallel.

4 · The proof — from the Results

The paper says

achieves 28.4 BLEU on the WMT 2014 English-to-German translation task

Vaswani et al., 2017

What it means

BLEU is a translation-quality score (higher = better). The simpler, all-attention model didn't just keep up — it beat the best systems in the world by over 2 BLEU, including ensembles (committees of older models ganged together). On French it hit 41.8, a new single-model record.

And because every word is processed in parallel, it trained in 3.5 days on 8 GPUs — a small fraction of what the previous champions cost. Better and cheaper: that's why everyone switched.

Key terms, in one line each

Attention — each word weighs how much every other word matters to it, and blends accordingly.
Query / Key / Value — what a word looks for, what it advertises, and what it contributes.
Multi-head — running attention 8 times in parallel, each head tracking a different relationship.
Positional encoding — since nothing reads in order, each word gets a position "timestamp" added in.
Encoder / decoder — the reader half that digests the input, and the writer half that produces the output.
BLEU — a translation quality score comparing machine output to human reference translations.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

The paper itself is about translation — the chatbot era came from scaling this blueprint up enormously (GPT, 2018 onward). Attention also has a real cost: every word attending to every word grows quadratically with length, which is why long context is expensive. And one elegance note: with no reading order, the model gets word positions from added sine-wave patterns — the "positional encodings" above.

Should you read the full paper?

Read it if…you want to understand modern AI at the root — it's short, unusually clearly written, and everything since is a footnote to it.
Skim is enough if…you mainly need the mechanism — the four translations above plus the attention slide carry the core idea.
Skip it if…you only use AI tools day-to-day; the blueprint matters less than what was built on it.
