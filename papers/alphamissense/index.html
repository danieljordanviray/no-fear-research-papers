AlphaMissense, translated

Author: Daniel Jordan Viray

Cheng et al., Science 381 (6664), 2023 · science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adg7492 · code: github.com/deepmind/alphamissense

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can see the evidence instead of taking its word for it.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

Your DNA is a recipe book, and proteins are the machines built from the recipes. A missense variant is a one-letter typo that swaps one building block of a protein for a different one. Some typos are harmless; some break the machine and cause disease. Doctors have found over 4 million such typos — but for 98% of them, nobody knows whether they're harmful. Those mysteries are the biggest bottleneck in genetic diagnosis. AlphaMissense is a spell-checker for these typos. It learned which typos nature tolerates (typos common in healthy people and primates) versus typos never seen anywhere (suspicious), combined with AlphaFold's knowledge of protein shapes — and now scores all 71 million possible typos. On every independent test, it beat the previous best methods.

The translation

1 · The problem — from the Introduction

The paper says

only an estimated 2% have been clinically classified as pathogenic or benign

Cheng et al., Science 2023 — on the 4M+ observed missense variants

What it means

Scientists have spotted 4 million+ one-letter typos in human DNA. For only 2 in 100 can doctors say "this one causes disease" (pathogenic) or "this one's harmless" (benign).

The other 98 are mysteries — officially "variants of uncertain significance" (VUS). When a genetic test finds one in you, the doctor has to shrug. That pile of mysteries is the single biggest bottleneck in genetic diagnosis.

2 · The trick — from the Methods

The paper says

fine-tuned AlphaFold on human and primate variant population frequency data

Cheng et al., Science 2023

What it means

They took AlphaFold — the AI that predicts a protein's 3D shape — and retrained it on a clever, completely free signal.

A typo that shows up often in healthy humans and primates is almost certainly harmless — its carriers lived fine. A typo never observed anywhere is suspicious. That's millions of training labels from an experiment evolution already ran — no doctor's opinions, no answer key, no circularity.

3 · The result — from the Results

The paper says

area under the receiver operator curve (auROC) of 0.940 on 18,924 ClinVar test variants

Cheng et al., Science 2023

What it means

Time for the fair exam: 18,924 typos doctors had already classified — which the model never trained on. It ranked the harmful typo above the harmless one 94% of the time, beating every previous method.

(That 0.940 is the score: 0.50 is a coin flip, 1.00 is perfect. And because the model never saw the answer key, this isn't cheating — most competitors can't say that.)

4 · The payoff — the community resource

The paper says

classify 32% of all missense variants as likely pathogenic and 57% as likely benign

Cheng et al., Science 2023

What it means

They then scored every possible typo in the human genome — 71 million of them — and gave the answers away free: roughly a third look harmful, over half look harmless.

The 98% mystery pile becomes a searchable database any clinic or lab can use. That's the product, not just the paper.

Key terms, in one line each

Missense variant — a one-letter DNA typo that swaps one protein building block for another.
Pathogenic / benign — the typo causes disease / the typo is harmless.
VUS — "variant of uncertain significance": a typo nobody has classified yet.
auROC — how often the model ranks a harmful typo above a harmless one; 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.
ClinVar — the public answer key: typos doctors have already classified.
MAVE — lab experiments that build thousands of mutated proteins and measure what breaks.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

Weaker on floppy, unstructured protein regions. Scores in the middle (0.2–0.8) are less reliable than the extremes. DeepMind released the 71M predictions but not the model weights — you get the answers, not the machine.

Should you read the full paper?

Read it if…you work on variant interpretation, precision medicine, or protein ML — the training-label design (weak labels from population frequency, self-distillation) is the transferable idea.
Skim is enough if…you just needed the idea and the evidence bar — the four translations above plus the data slides carry ~90% of it.
Skip it if…you don't touch genetics. The architecture details only matter if you're building on it.
