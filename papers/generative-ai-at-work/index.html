Generative AI at Work, translated

Author: Daniel Jordan Viray

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, Quarterly Journal of Economics 140 (2): 889–942, 2025 · nber.org — working paper & abstract
The first large field study of generative AI on real jobs.

How to read this page: on the left, the paper's own words — short excerpts, exactly as written. On the right, the same idea in everyday English. Data slides appear where the paper makes a claim, so you can see exactly who gained, and who didn't.

The whole paper in 60 seconds

A Fortune 500 software firm rolled out an AI assistant to its customer-support agents — a tool trained on the company's own best support conversations, suggesting replies and links in real time. Because the rollout was staggered across 5,179 agents, the researchers could measure its effect almost like an experiment. Headline: agents with AI resolved 14% more issues per hour. But the average hides the story: novices gained 34%, while top performers gained almost nothing. The mechanism is the beautiful part — the AI had absorbed the know-how of the firm's best agents and now whispered it to everyone else. Customers were happier, agents quit less, and new hires climbed the learning curve faster.

The translation

1 · The setup — from the Abstract

The paper says

the staggered introduction of a generative AI-based conversational assistant using data from 5,179 customer support agents

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE 2025

What it means

Not a lab demo — a real deployment. Thousands of chat-support agents got the AI at different times (a staggered rollout, for operational reasons), and that accident is what makes the study credible: agents with the tool can be compared against similar agents not yet given it, before and after — the workhorse design of modern causal inference.

The AI itself: trained on millions of past support chats, it suggests responses and documentation as the conversation unfolds. The agent stays in charge and can edit or ignore.

2 · The headline — from the Abstract

The paper says

Access to the tool increases productivity, as measured by issues resolved per hour, by 14% on average

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE 2025

What it means

Fourteen percent more customer problems solved per hour, without a matching drop in quality — resolution rates and customer satisfaction also held or improved. For a mature, heavily-optimized operation, a double-digit productivity jump from a software rollout is enormous.

But "on average" is doing heavy lifting. Keep reading.

Data slide · who actually gained?

Change in issues resolved per hour after getting the AI, by worker type (paper, abstract):

Novice & low-skilled agents+34%
All agents (average)+14%
Experienced & highly skilled~0%

Most technologies of the last forty years did the opposite — they amplified the already-skilled ("skill-biased technical change"). This one compressed the distribution from below.

3 · The mechanism — from the Abstract

The paper says

the AI model disseminates the best practices of more able workers

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE 2025

What it means

Why would novices gain most? Because of what the model was trained on: the firm's archive of successful conversations — disproportionately the work of its best agents. Their tacit know-how (how to phrase a workaround, when to escalate, what to check first) got baked into the suggestions every agent now sees.

In effect, the company bottled its top performers' judgment and distributed it at zero marginal cost. Novices suddenly perform as if they've been trained by the best — because, through the model, they have been.

4 · The side effects — from the Abstract

The paper says

AI assistance improves customer sentiment, increases employee retention, and may lead to worker learning

Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE 2025

What it means

The gains weren't squeezed out of people — the whole experience improved. Customers were politer to agents (sentiment analysis of the chats), fewer customers demanded a manager, and agents — especially newer ones — quit less often. And "learning": newer agents with AI climbed the experience curve faster, suggesting they were absorbing skills, not just renting them.

Key terms, in one line each

Staggered rollout — introducing a treatment to different groups at different times; the timing gaps let researchers isolate cause from coincidence.
Experience curve — the path of getting better with tenure; AI steepened it for new agents.
Skill-biased technical change — the old pattern: new tech raises demand for skilled workers most. This study found the reverse gradient.
Tacit knowledge — know-how experts can't easily write down; here it leaked into the training data and became transferable.
Productivity (here) — issues resolved per hour, checked against quality and customer-satisfaction measures.
Attrition — workers quitting; it fell, particularly among agents with the least tenure.

Fine print (what the translation shouldn't hide)

One firm, one job, one tool — customer support chat is unusually measurable and unusually well-matched to what language models do. Whether the novice-boost generalizes to, say, surgery or strategy is an open question. The top agents' slight dip on some quality measures deserves attention too: if the best workers' output trains the model that then compresses their advantage, the long-run incentive to become excellent is a real design problem. And productivity is not wages — who pockets the 14% is a bargaining question, not a measurement.

Should you read the full paper?

Read it if…you think about AI and work for a living — this is the reference field study, and its design (staggered rollout as natural experiment) is a template worth stealing.
Skim is enough if…you need the shape: +14% average, +34% novices, ~0 experts, mechanism = bottled best practice.
Skip it if…you're after frontier-model capabilities rather than workplace evidence — the Noy & Zhang experiment (also on this site) is the quicker read.
